Comets

Icy wanderers from the outer solar system that grow spectacular tails near the Sun.

What Is a Comet?

A comet is a small solar-system body composed mainly of ice, dust, and rocky material — often described as a “dirty snowball.” Most comets originate in the cold outer regions: the Kuiper Belt (beyond Neptune) or the distant Oort Cloud.

When a comet’s orbit brings it close to the Sun, the ices heat up and sublimate (turn directly from solid to gas). This releases dust and gas that form a glowing coma (atmosphere) around the nucleus and, often, one or more long tails.

Structure of a Comet

  • Nucleus – The solid core, typically a few kilometres across.
  • Coma – The temporary atmosphere of gas and dust that can grow to tens of thousands of kilometres wide.
  • Dust tail – Curved, yellowish; made of dust particles pushed by sunlight.
  • Ion (gas) tail – Straight, bluish; charged particles swept back by the solar wind.

Both tails always point generally away from the Sun, so a comet can appear to travel “tail-first” when leaving the inner solar system.

Famous Comets

Halley’s Comet

The most famous periodic comet with an orbital period of ~76 years. Last perihelion was in 1986, and it is expected to return in 2061.

Comet Hale–Bopp (C/1995 O1)

One of the brightest comets of the 20th century, visible to the naked eye for many months during 1996–1997.

Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9

Broke apart and collided with Jupiter in 1994, providing a dramatic, real-time demonstration of cosmic impacts.

Interstellar Visitors

Objects like 1I/‘Oumuamua and 2I/Borisov originated outside our solar system, providing vital clues about other planetary systems.

Why Comets Matter

  • They preserve primitive material from the early formation of the solar system.
  • Early impacts may have delivered water and organic compounds to Earth.
  • They are prime targets for deep-space exploration (e.g., ESA’s Rosetta mission).
  • Bright comets remain among the most spectacular naked-eye astronomical events.

Constellations

Patterns of stars that have guided humans for thousands of years.

What Is a Constellation?

A constellation is a recognized pattern of stars in the night sky, often named after mythological figures, animals, or objects. The stars in a constellation usually lie at very different distances from Earth; they only appear close together from our viewpoint.

The International Astronomical Union (IAU) officially recognizes 88 constellations that together cover the entire celestial sphere. These boundaries were standardized in the early 20th century so that every point in the sky belongs to exactly one constellation.

How Are Constellations Formed?

Constellations are cultural and historical constructs, not physical groupings. Ancient peoples connected bright stars into familiar shapes to help remember the sky, mark seasons, and navigate. Different cultures saw different patterns (for example, the Greek “Orion” versus various Indigenous asterisms).

Modern astronomy uses the 88 official constellations as a convenient coordinate system. Within them, we also speak of asterisms — informal star patterns such as the Big Dipper (part of Ursa Major) or the Summer Triangle.

Notable Constellations

Ursa Major (The Great Bear)

Contains the famous Big Dipper asterism. Visible year-round from mid-northern latitudes. Its two outer bowl stars point directly to Polaris, the North Star.

Orion (The Hunter)

Iconic constellation featuring Betelgeuse and Rigel. Orion's Belt points down toward the Orion Nebula, an active star-forming region.

Cassiopeia

A distinctive “W” or “M” shape in the northern sky, named after a queen in Greek mythology. Helps locate the north celestial pole.

Scorpius (The Scorpion)

A southern summer constellation featuring a curved hook tail and the bright red supergiant Antares (“rival of Mars”).

Crux (The Southern Cross)

The smallest of the 88 constellations, yet iconic in the Southern Hemisphere. Points toward the south celestial pole and appears on multiple flags.

Leo (The Lion)

A prominent spring constellation in the northern sky. Features the “Sickle” asterism and the bright star Regulus.

Why Constellations Still Matter

  • Essential for visual navigation and sky orientation under dark skies.
  • Historical seasonal markers for agriculture and cultural calendars.
  • A universal coordinate system for astronomers to locate celestial objects.
  • An accessible entryway for beginners learning observational astronomy.
